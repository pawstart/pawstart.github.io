12 Signs Your Dog Needs a Vet Visit, Not a Guess

Dogs are terrible patients — not because they complain, but because they don't. A dog's instinct is to look fine even when something is wrong, so by the time a problem is obvious it has often been building quietly for a while. Due to a dog's survival instinct, they will work to appear healthy on the outside, so it's not always easy to know when they need help.

This guide covers the 12 observable signs veterinarians say should trigger a phone call or a trip to the clinic — and how urgent each one is. One rule runs through all of them: this is general information, not medical advice, and it never replaces your veterinarian. You know your dog better than anyone, so if something seems wrong, give your vet a call. When in doubt, call. Vets would far rather answer a "probably nothing" call than see a dog that waited too long.

How urgent is it? The three tiers

Not every sign means the same response. Sort what you're seeing into one of these tiers:

TierWhat it meansExamples
Go nowEmergency — leave for the vet or ER clinic immediatelyTrouble breathing, collapse, seizure, major trauma, suspected poisoning
Call todayNeeds a vet's judgment within hoursRepeated vomiting, bloody stool, sudden severe pain, won't urinate
Monitor, then callWatch closely; call if it persists past the stated windowSkipped one meal, mild lethargy under two days, soft stool once

The list below is ordered roughly from most urgent to least — but any sign paired with another sign jumps a tier. Vomiting plus lethargy is more urgent than either alone. Vomiting together with lethargy, a poor appetite, and diarrhea can be an emergency — contact your veterinarian immediately.

The 12 signs

1. Trouble breathing

Faster or harder breathing than normal while at rest, noisy or strained breaths, gums or tongue turning blue, the neck stretched out to draw breath, or the belly working hard with each breath. Breathing trouble of any kind counts as an emergency. Go now — don't wait to see if it eases.

2. Sudden collapse, weakness, or can't use their legs

A dog that suddenly collapses, goes very weak, or loses the use of one or more legs needs to be seen right away. This is a go-now sign even if they seem to recover a minute later — the episode itself is the warning.

3. A seizure

Uncontrolled shaking or paddling, loss of awareness, drooling. Seizure activity is listed among the emergent conditions by Cornell's emergency service. Time it if you can, keep your hands away from their mouth, clear nearby hazards — and call the vet or ER clinic immediately. Even a single first seizure warrants a veterinary exam, and clusters of seizures close together or one lasting more than five minutes mean emergency care. Seizures, sudden collapse, and difficulty breathing are all reasons to get to a vet or emergency clinic without delay.

4. Repeated vomiting

The odd single vomit after eating too fast is common. The line is repetition: vomiting again and again within a single day, especially alongside blood, lethargy, or refusing food. Repeated vomiting or vomiting blood belongs in the call-today tier at minimum — vomiting blood moves it to go-now.

5. Diarrhea lasting more than a day — or with blood

Diarrhea for more than 24 hours deserves a vet call, and blood or mucus in the stool, or dark tarry stools that may indicate blood, should be reported right away. If you can, bring a fresh stool sample to the appointment — it genuinely helps the diagnosis.

6. Won't eat for a day or more

Many dogs skip a meal and bounce back. The AKC's guidance: call your veterinarian if an adult dog refuses food while vomiting, refuses food while having diarrhea, has both vomiting and diarrhea, or shows a major or lasting change in appetite. With young puppies, any missed meal may be concerning — a puppy refusing food should be reported sooner, not later. (New puppy? Our first-30-days checklist covers what those early vet visits look like.)

7. Lethargy that lasts more than two days

A tired dog after a long hike is normal. A dog that's unusually tired and sluggish — no interest in play or walks, slower to respond than normal — for over two days should be seen. Lethargy is one of the easiest signs to wave off and one of the most common early warnings, so put a mental 48-hour timer on it.

8. Sudden or unexplained weight loss

Unexplained weight loss in a dog of any size is a reason to see the veterinarian — flag it if you notice roughly a ten percent drop. Gradual slimming on a diet plan is one thing; weight falling off an otherwise normally-fed dog is a call-today sign.

9. Clear signs of pain

Dogs often hide pain, so watch for behavior changes: a limp, panting, eating less, trouble moving, restlessness, or pulling back from activities they used to enjoy. Pain that comes on suddenly or severely needs immediate veterinary attention; slower, gradual changes still deserve a veterinary evaluation. And one hard rule: don't give your dog human pain medication unless a veterinary professional has guided you — common human painkillers can be dangerous for dogs.

10. Straining to urinate or defecate — or blood in either

Inability to urinate or defecate is a call-today sign at minimum, and bleeding from the mouth, nose, or eyes, or blood in vomit, urine, or stool should be reported promptly. Straining with little or nothing coming out, especially repeated trips outside with no result, shouldn't wait for morning.

11. Pale, white, or blue gums

Lift the lip and look. Healthy gums are pink; pale or blue gums are a warning sign worth an urgent call. It's a 5-second check you can do at home any time something feels off — useful information to report when you phone the clinic.

12. Ate something potentially toxic

Chocolate, xylitol (also labeled birch sugar) in sugar-free gum and sweets, grapes and raisins, human medications — if your dog has swallowed something toxic or an object that could block the gut — a toy, a sock — you usually need to move fast. Don't wait for symptoms. Call your vet or an emergency clinic right away; for a suspected toxin you can also contact the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435, which can help determine whether an emergency visit is necessary. Don't try to make your dog vomit or give a home remedy unless a vet or poison-control professional has told you to.

What to tell the vet when you call

You don't need a diagnosis — that's their job. What helps them triage you fast is a tight, factual report. Use this 60-second script:

  1. What you see, plainly: "He's vomited four times since last night" beats "I think he has food poisoning."
  2. When it started and whether it's getting worse, better, or holding steady.
  3. The big three: is he eating, drinking, and going to the bathroom normally?
  4. Anything he got into: new food, trash, plants, chemicals, medications — even if you're not sure.
  5. What he's taking: current medications and when the last dose was.

Then ask the only question that matters: "Do we need to come in now, or can this wait until morning?" Let them make that call — it's what triage is for.

Save these numbers before you need them

Do this today, while nothing is wrong. It's a good idea to have the contact information for your closest emergency animal hospital — which may be different from your regular vet — at the ready, so you don't have to take time to look it up if an emergency does happen. Save three numbers in your phone: your regular vet, the nearest 24-hour emergency animal hospital, and the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435). Wondering what vet care actually costs? Our first-year cost breakdown and honest guide to pet insurance cover the money side, and the puppy vaccination schedule explains why young dogs see the vet so often in the first place.

A final note

None of this is about becoming an anxious helicopter owner. Most skipped meals resolve, most soft stools firm up, most tired dogs perk back up. The skill isn't spotting every symptom — it's knowing which ones have a clock on them. Breathing trouble, collapse, seizures, repeated vomiting, and suspected poisoning don't get a "wait and see." Everything else gets your judgment, your vet's phone number, and the 48-hour rule. That's the whole system.

This article is general information for new dog owners, not veterinary advice. If something seems off, ask your veterinarian — only they can examine your dog, diagnose a problem, or recommend treatment.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if it's an emergency or can wait until morning?

Use the tiers above. Any trouble breathing is an emergency, and seizure activity is listed among the emergent conditions — those go now, no waiting. Vomiting together with lethargy, a poor appetite, and diarrhea can be an emergency — contact your veterinarian immediately. For everything else, call your vet and let their triage decide — that phone call is exactly what the "can this wait until morning?" question is for.

My dog vomited once — should I call the vet?

Probably not yet, if that's the only sign and your dog is otherwise acting normally — the odd single vomit after eating too fast is common. The line is repetition: vomiting again and again within a single day, especially alongside blood, lethargy, or refusing food, move it to call-today at minimum. Vomiting blood moves it to go-now. If the single vomit comes with lethargy, appetite loss, or diarrhea, treat it as urgent.

Can I give my dog human painkillers?

No — not without veterinary guidance. Don't give your dog human pain medication unless a veterinary professional has guided you — common human painkillers can be dangerous for dogs. If you see signs of pain — a limp, panting, trouble moving, restlessness, or pulling back from activities they used to enjoy — call your vet and describe what you see; they can advise what's safe for your dog's size and situation.

What should I do if my dog eats something toxic?

Don't wait for symptoms. If your dog has swallowed something toxic or an object that could block the gut — a toy, a sock — you usually need to move fast — call your vet or an emergency clinic right away, or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435. Don't try to make your dog vomit or give a home remedy unless a vet or poison-control professional has told you to — the wrong home treatment can make things worse.

How do I check my dog's gums, and what should they look like?

Gently lift the lip and look at the gums above the teeth. Healthy gums are pink; pale or blue gums are a warning sign worth an urgent call. It's a quick check you can do any time something feels off, and it's useful information to report when you phone the clinic — along with what you see, when it started, and whether your dog is eating, drinking, and going to the bathroom normally.
