New Puppy Checklist: The First 30 Days, Week by Week

The first 30 days with a new puppy are equal parts wonderful and bewildering. One minute you're melting over a sleeping fluffball; the next you're mopping the floor at 3 a.m. wondering what you've done. Every new puppy owner lives some version of this month — and it gets better faster than you'd think, as long as the first weeks are handled right.

This checklist walks you through it week by week. You'll learn what to do before pickup day, how to survive the first 72 hours, when to build routines, when to see the vet, and how to use the socialization window while it's open. One principle runs through all of it, borrowed from shelter professionals: the first month is about settling, routine, and trust — not perfection. The widely used 3-3-3 guideline puts it plainly: the first 3 days a new pet is stressed and overwhelmed, the next 3 weeks they start to settle in, and it takes around 3 months to fully feel at home. Your job in month one is simply to make weeks two and three better than week one.

Before pickup day: the 10-minute safety sweep

Supplies are covered in our full shopping guide — What to Buy Before Bringing a Puppy Home: The Honest Checklist — so this section is about safety only. Puppies explore the world with their mouths, and your home is full of things that are dangerous to chew. The ASPCA's Animal Poison Control Center recommends a quick sweep before your puppy arrives: remove poisonous houseplants or move them out of reach, lock all medications in an enclosed cupboard, and remove any ground-level insecticide or rodenticide baits.

  • Countertops: don't leave food, medications, cleaning supplies, or candles on counters — a motivated puppy will find a way up.
  • Garbage cans: keep every garbage can covered or stored where the puppy can't reach it.
  • Cleaning supplies: household cleaners and laundry detergents belong in an enclosed space, inaccessible to pets.
  • Small objects: dogs readily eat small children's toys, which can cause intestinal blockage — pick them up and keep them away from snooping noses.
  • Candles and cords: relocate candles a wagging tail could knock over, and lift electrical cords off the floor.

Ten minutes now saves a panicked vet call later. If you ever suspect your puppy swallowed something toxic, contact your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center immediately — and ask your veterinarian to confirm the right emergency number for your area.

Days 1–3: the decompression window

Your puppy just lost everything familiar — their mother, their littermates, every smell they knew. In these first 3 days, expect stress and possible fear or anxiety. The prescription is simple: get into a routine, be patient and stay positive, give calm clear direction, and give them time to observe without forcing interactions. Don't expect the playful pup from the breeder's videos yet. Expect a tired, confused baby who needs naps more than entertainment.

On arrival day itself, keep the world small. The AKC advises limiting your puppy's access at first — too many new places, smells, and people at once can confuse them. Let them explore one designated area with you, then introduce the rest of the house one room at a time. First order of business: take them to the outside spot where you want them to eliminate, use a command you'll stick with (like "go potty"), and reward with a special treat and praise when they go.

Introductions matter more than you'd think. Meet family members one person at a time, quietly — supervise young children and discourage them from picking the puppy up. Don't invite friends and neighbors over on the first day; that can start soon, but not today. And one rule with no exceptions: until your puppy has completed their vaccinations, they shouldn't interact with unfamiliar dogs or walk where unknown dogs go.

Two admin tasks for the first 72 hours: book a veterinary checkup within the next few days, and start enforcing your house rules from day one — where the puppy sleeps, whether furniture is allowed, and who's in charge of what. Praise good behavior and set rules ahead of time so the whole household stays consistent.

Week 1: routine is everything

By the end of the first week, your puppy should be able to predict their day — and that predictability is what makes them feel safe. Structure helps a new puppy feel secure and understand what's expected, and it makes life easier for the humans too. Build the day around four anchors: meals, potty, play, and sleep.

Meals: young puppies eat three times a day — put them on a regular feeding schedule, because what goes in on a schedule comes out on a schedule. Feed the same food the breeder or shelter was using for at least the first week; a sudden diet change on top of a new home is a recipe for an upset stomach. If you plan to switch foods, ask your veterinarian how to transition gradually.

Potty: take your puppy out every time they eat, drink, wake up, play, or start sniffing around — most young puppies need to eliminate at least every 45 minutes when awake. Carry them to the spot so they don't get distracted en route. Never punish housetraining mistakes; when they go in the right spot, reward them immediately. Accidents are a scheduling failure, not a puppy failure.

Sleep: young puppies sleep a lot — some 16 to 18 hours a day — so plan several nap times and consider a crate in a quiet part of the house where they won't be disturbed. An overtired puppy is a bitey, zoomy, miserable puppy. Enforced naps are not optional in week one.

The first night deserves its own plan, because it's usually the hardest. Our full guide is here: How to Crate Train a Puppy at Night (Without the 2 A.M. Concert). The short version from the AKC: put the crate in your bedroom so the puppy feels secure with you nearby, add a blanket or toy that smells like their mother, and set a consistent bedtime. If they whine at night, carry them out for a quick, boring potty break — then straight back in the crate. And decide now: don't bring a crying puppy into your bed unless that's where you want them sleeping permanently.

Week 2: the real personality arrives

Something shifts in week two. The fog lifts, the appetite shows up, and you start meeting the actual dog — playful, curious, opinionated, and suddenly very interested in testing boundaries. This is the 3-3-3 "next 3 weeks" phase: pets start to settle in and show more of their personality, which is exactly when you should work on basic training (sit, come, wait), positively reinforce every success, and stay consistent as they test the rules. Boundary-testing isn't defiance; it's a puppy learning how the world works.

Start training in tiny sessions — two to three minutes, a few times a day. Name recognition, sit, and come are plenty for week two. Keep it fun and end every session with a win. If you haven't already, this is also the week to begin the handling exercises that pay off for years: touch their paws, ears, and mouth gently during calm moments, paired with treats.

The first vet visit should happen this week if it hasn't already. Here's what it covers, so nothing surprises you: the vet will weigh the puppy, listen to the heart and lungs, and take their temperature. They will examine the eyes, ears, nose, feet, and genitalia, check the skin and coat, look at the teeth and mouth, palpate the abdomen and lymph nodes, and examine a fecal sample for worms. Bring all paperwork from the breeder or rescue — medical records, vaccination history, and deworming records — so the vet can build a complete file. Use the visit to discuss feeding, worm medications, microchipping, spay/neuter timing, and to set up the schedule for follow-up visits and vaccinations. Our deeper guide to the vaccine timeline is here: Puppy Vaccination Schedule: What Every New Owner Should Know. And as always: if anything about your puppy's health worries you, ask your veterinarian — don't wait for the next scheduled visit.

Week 3: the socialization window

Week three is when the calendar matters most. The critical social development period for dogs runs approximately 3 to 14 weeks — this is when puppies are most accepting, curious, and least cautious about their environment, and socialization after it closes is a slower, harder process. Most puppies come home at 8 weeks, which means the 8-to-12-week stretch is also the puppy "fear period": they're becoming wary of new experiences just as they're at their most impressionable. Positive experiences now shape them profoundly — and this is when they form their strongest attachments to their people. In other words: week three is prime time, and it doesn't come back.

Socialization doesn't mean greeting every dog at the park. It means calm, positive exposure to the sights, sounds, surfaces, people, and handling of everyday life. Keep experiences positive and controlled, and from day one get your puppy used to being handled — look in their ears, feet, and mouth — which makes future groomer and vet visits far less stressful. Good week-three exposures: different floor textures, umbrellas opening, doorbells, calm visitors of different ages, car rides, and the vacuum cleaner running in the next room.

The golden rule: watch the puppy, not the checklist. Learn the stress signals — tucked tail, licking lips, yawning, shaking, ears back — and if you see them, remove the puppy before they're overwhelmed so the experience doesn't get filed under "scary." One calm five-minute exposure beats a forced half hour every time. And remember the vaccination boundary: puppies that haven't completed their shots shouldn't mix with dogs of unknown vaccination status or walk in potentially contaminated areas like dog parks. Carry them through busy areas, or arrange playdates with healthy, vaccinated dogs you know.

Week 4: it starts to feel like a routine

By week four, the chaos has a shape. The puppy knows when meals happen, where the potty spot is, and that the crate means sleep. Accidents are fewer (not gone — that takes months, and that's normal). Naps are predictable. You're getting the hang of reading their signals, and they're getting the hang of you. This is the payoff for three weeks of consistency.

Two things ramp up now. First, chewing: as permanent teeth start emerging, chewing becomes one of the main ways puppies explore, so chew toys become a household necessity. Redirect relentlessly — every shoe saved is a habit not formed. If the nipping is getting intense, our guide covers it: Puppy Biting? How to Stop the Nipping Without the Drama. Second, confidence: from around 12 weeks puppies grow less fearful and more curious and independent, and start figuring out their place in the family. By 16 weeks they're ready for training classes, solid house-training, and basic commands like "come." You're building toward that — keep sessions short, fun, and consistent.

End the month with an honest review using the milestone checklist below. Whatever isn't checked yet isn't a failure — it's next month's work. Remember the full 3-3-3 arc: at 30 days you're one-third of the way to a fully settled dog, and the trust you're building now is the foundation everything else stands on.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take a puppy to adjust to a new home?

Expect visible settling within 3 weeks, but the 3-3-3 guideline says the first 3 days are decompression, the next 3 weeks are settling in, and it takes about 3 months on average for a pet to fully acclimate — some need longer, some adapt faster. If your puppy still seems shut down or fearful after a month, ask your veterinarian whether something more is going on.

When should I take my new puppy to the vet?

Book the first checkup within the first few days of bringing your puppy home. Bring all breeder or rescue paperwork — vaccination history, deworming records, and medical notes — plus a fresh fecal sample if you can manage one. This visit sets the baseline for everything: vaccine schedule, parasite prevention, microchipping, and spay/neuter timing.

How much should my puppy sleep in the first month?

A lot more than you'd guess. Young puppies sleep 16 to 18 hours a day, so plan several naps and don't mistake a cranky, bitey evening puppy for a naughty one — they're usually just overtired. Protect nap time the way you'd protect a toddler's.

When can my puppy meet other dogs?

Not until vaccinations are further along. Puppies without complete vaccinations shouldn't interact with unfamiliar dogs or walk where unknown dogs go. That doesn't mean zero socialization — the 3-to-14-week window is critical, so use safe exposure instead: calm people, new sounds and surfaces, and playdates with healthy vaccinated dogs you know. Ask your veterinarian when your specific puppy is cleared for wider contact.

My puppy cries at night — is that normal?

Completely normal in the first week — they've just left their mother and littermates. Keep the crate in your bedroom so they feel your presence nearby, and answer nighttime whining with a quick, boring potty trip — not playtime, and not a move to your bed (unless that's the permanent plan). Some puppies sleep through the night right away; others cry for the first few nights — a consistent bedtime routine is what settles it. Our full nighttime guide walks through it step by step: How to Crate Train a Puppy at Night.
