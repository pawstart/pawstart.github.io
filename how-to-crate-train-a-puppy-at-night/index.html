How to Crate Train a Puppy at Night (Without the 2 A.M. Concert)

The first night with a new puppy usually goes like this: you put them in the crate, turn off the light, climb into bed feeling hopeful — and then the crying starts. Here's the short answer to what you're wondering: yes, keep the crate in your bedroom for the first few nights. Yes, some crying on night one and two is completely normal. And if your puppy wakes up crying at 2 a.m., they probably need to pee — take them out, keep it boring, and put them straight back to bed. This guide walks you through the whole plan, night by night.

Before the first night: set the crate up for success

Nighttime crate training goes much better when your puppy has already decided the crate is a good place during the day. A little prep work now saves you a lot of crying later. If you haven't bought the gear yet, our puppy shopping checklist covers exactly what you need before day one.

Picking the right crate size

Your puppy should be able to stand up, turn around, and lie down comfortably — and that's it. A crate that's too big causes a specific problem: your puppy can pee in one corner and sleep in the other, which teaches them exactly the habit you're trying to avoid. If you bought a crate your puppy will grow into, use the divider panel to shrink the living space for now.

Where to put the crate the first week

Right next to your bed. Sleeping the first few nights with the crate close by — where your puppy can smell, hear, and see you — makes the transition to sleeping alone much easier. You can move the crate a little farther away each night once your puppy is settling well. There's a practical bonus too: with the crate in your bedroom, you'll hear when your puppy wakes up and needs a potty break.

Make the crate cozy before the first night: soft bedding, a safe chew toy, and optionally a lightweight blanket draped over the top to make it feel den-like. The single most important rule, according to the American Kennel Club, is that your puppy always connects the crate with a positive experience — never use it as punishment.

Do a daytime practice run first

If your puppy's very first crate experience is eight hours overnight, you're asking a lot. Spend the day before (or even a few days before) building positive associations:

  • Toss treats into the crate and let your puppy wander in on their own. Praise and reward every time they go in. The Humane Society Silicon Valley suggests repeating treat tosses about ten times with a cue like "go to bed".
  • Feed a meal or two inside the crate with the door open.
  • Practice short naps in the crate — start with about ten minutes and build up — with a stuffed Kong or chew toy for company.
  • Close the door for brief periods while you're nearby, then open it calmly. Give treats while your puppy is inside the crate, and when you let them out, just open the door and walk away quietly — no big celebration for coming out.

The first-night routine, step by step

Puppies thrive on routine. Doing the same calm sequence every night teaches your puppy what "bedtime" means faster than anything else.

Your bedtime checklist

  1. Last potty break right before bed. Same spot, same cue word, praise when they go.
  2. Wind down. No wild play in the last 30 minutes. Calm energy from you sets the tone.
  3. Cue and treat. Say your crate cue ("go to bed," "crate time" — pick one and stick with it), toss a treat inside, and let your puppy walk in.
  4. Add the good stuff. A stuffed Kong or safe chew toy goes in with them. Reserve special high-value chews for crate time only, so the crate stays associated with the best things.
  5. Lights off, settle in. At night, ask your puppy to go in, turn off the lights, and settle yourself to sleep. The first night, you can rest your hand near the crate or talk softly until they doze off.

The boring midnight potty trip

Your puppy will probably wake you up. That's good — it means they're asking instead of having an accident. Here's the drill: carry or leash them straight to the potty spot, use your cue word, praise quietly when they go, then straight back into the crate. This should be a trip with a purpose, not playtime — take them out, reward the potty, and immediately put them back to bed. No cuddles, no play session, no midnight snack. Boring is the whole point: you want your puppy to learn that nighttime wake-ups lead only back to sleep.

How long can your puppy actually hold it?

This is the question that decides whether you set an alarm or wait to be woken up. The AKC's rule of thumb: take your puppy's age in months and add one — that's roughly the number of hours they can hold it. So a two-month-old puppy maxes out around three hours.

Most puppies can sleep through the night without a potty break by about four months of age — but that's an average, not a promise. Small breeds and individual puppies vary, so when in doubt, take them out.

Whining vs. needing to pee: how to tell the difference

Not all crying means the same thing, and responding correctly is the skill that makes crate training click. Here's how to read it:

  • The timing test. If your puppy cries in the middle of the night after sleeping quietly, they're likely whining because they need the bathroom. If they start crying the moment the lights go off, that's usually protest — they're testing whether crying gets them out.
  • The potty-phrase test. Quietly say your outside potty cue. An excited, wiggly response means "yes, I need to go." No reaction means it's probably protest crying.
  • The 3-second quiet rule. If you do open the crate door, wait until your puppy is calm and quiet for a brief moment first — otherwise they learn that whining is the button that opens the door.

For protest whining, the standard approach across humane societies is consistent: ignore the whining until your puppy is quiet, then reward the quiet. Giving in teaches them to cry longer and louder next time. But there's an important exception: if the crying keeps escalating for 20–30 minutes instead of fading, or your puppy seems panicked rather than annoyed, stop and go back a few steps in your daytime training. A puppy that is truly distressed isn't learning anything.

The 7-night plan: what progress looks like

  1. Nights 1–2: Crate right next to your bed. Expect crying — the AKC notes puppies may cry the first night or two as they adjust to being away from their mom and littermates. Plan 1–2 boring potty breaks. Comfort them briefly, then lights out.
  2. Nights 3–4: The crying should get shorter each night. Keep the routine identical — same cue, same treat, same boring potty trips. Consistency is doing the work even when it doesn't feel like it.
  3. Nights 5–7: Once your puppy settles within a few minutes, start moving the crate a few feet farther from your bed each night toward its permanent spot.
  4. Week 2 and beyond: Most puppies now walk into the crate willingly at bedtime. Keep one planned potty break as long as the age math says they need it, then drop it when they're consistently dry until morning.

Mistakes that make night crating harder

  • Letting them out mid-cry. Even once teaches them that crying works — and they'll try it louder next time.
  • Making midnight potty trips fun. Play, cuddles, or treats at 2 a.m. turn wake-ups into something worth repeating.
  • Skipping daytime practice. Going from zero crate time straight to a full night is the most common reason the first week feels impossible.
  • Crating too long during the day too. Never leave a puppy in the crate all day — they need regular bathroom breaks, play, and feeding times. An overtired, under-exercised puppy is a louder nighttime puppy — and an overtired puppy is also a bitey one (see how to stop puppy biting).
  • Scolding or yelling at the crate. Punishment around the crate poisons the positive association you're building. If you're frustrated, step away and breathe — the puppy is not giving you a hard time, they're having a hard time.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Should the puppy's crate be in my bedroom?

Yes, for the first several nights. Both the NHSPCA and multiple humane societies recommend starting with the crate in your bedroom — your puppy feels secure, and you can hear genuine potty-need whining. Move it gradually once they're settling well.

How long will my puppy cry in the crate at night?

Most puppies cry the first one or two nights and improve quickly from there if you're consistent. If intense crying continues past a week despite steady training, go back to shorter daytime sessions and build up more gradually.

Should I ignore my puppy crying in the crate at night?

It depends on the cry. Mid-night waking after quiet sleep usually means a potty break — take them out. Immediate lights-off protest crying should be waited out (reward the quiet moments). When in doubt, take them out: a boring potty trip never hurt training, but an accident in the crate sets it back.

What time should a puppy go to bed?

Match your own bedtime, but keep it consistent within about 30 minutes each night. The last potty break should be immediately before crating — not an hour before.

When can my puppy sleep through the night without a potty break?

Most puppies manage it by about four months old, but use the age-in-months-plus-one rule to decide for your individual puppy, and keep a backup plan for nights when they're restless.
