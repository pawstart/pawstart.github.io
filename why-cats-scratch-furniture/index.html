Why Cats Scratch Furniture (and How to Redirect It)

If your couch looks like it's been attacked by a tiny lumberjack, here's the reframe that changes everything: your cat isn't being bad. Scratching is normal feline behavior — as natural to a cat as stretching is to you when you wake up. They scratch during play, when stretching, and to mark their territory.

That means the goal was never to stop the scratching. The best tactic isn't trying to stop your cat from scratching, but teaching them where and what to scratch. This guide shows you why the couch keeps winning — and the two-step method that actually redirects those claws.

It's not revenge: the 4 real reasons cats scratch

Once you understand what's driving the behavior, the solution gets obvious. Cats scratch for four overlapping reasons:

1. Claw maintenance. Cats scratch to remove frayed, worn outer claws and expose new, sharper ones underneath. It's grooming, not vandalism — like filing your nails, except the file is your armchair.

2. Full-body stretching. Watch a cat scratch and you'll see the whole body engage — back arched, legs extended, toes spread. Scratching flexes the feet, claws, and back muscles in one satisfying motion.

3. Territory marking. Cats scratch to mark territory — and they mark with more than claw marks. Cats have scent glands in their paws, so every scratch leaves both a visual signal and a scent signal that says "this is mine."

4. Stress release and play. Scratching ramps up with excitement and anxiety alike. A cat who scratches more after a move, a new pet, or a schedule change is often self-soothing the only way they know how.

Why the couch wins: what makes furniture irresistible

Your cat isn't choosing the couch to spite you. From a cat's perspective, your furniture is simply the best scratching post in the house:

  • It's sturdy and tall. Most cats like a post tall enough for a full stretch — and your sofa arm or drapes offer exactly that. (This may be why cats seem to like drapes so much.)
  • The texture is right. Upholstery weave gives the same satisfying resistance as tree bark. A flimsy cardboard scratcher from the discount bin can't compete.
  • It's prime territory. Furniture in social areas gets scratched more because it's central territory — the feline equivalent of putting your name on the conference room.
  • It's near the nap spot. Cats like to scratch when they wake up, so whatever stands nearest the favorite sleeping spot becomes the morning stretch station.

The takeaway: to beat the couch, you don't need discipline. You need to offer something that checks the same boxes — sturdy, tall, well-textured, well-placed.

Step 1: give them something better to scratch

This is where most people go wrong: they buy one scratching post, the cat ignores it, and they conclude "my cat just won't use one." The problem is almost never the cat. It's the post.

Matching the post to your cat

Provide a variety of scratching posts with different qualities and surfaces — cardboard, carpeting, wood, sisal, upholstery. Offer a variety of scratchers to figure out what your cat prefers: some lay flat, some stand upright, some tilt at an angle.

Watch what your cat already chooses and match it. Vertical couch-scratcher? They want a tall upright post. Carpet-clawer? They want horizontal. The furniture is market research — use it.

Two non-negotiables: the post must not wobble or tip over (a post that shifts mid-scratch gets permanently rejected), and it should be tall enough for that full stretch.

Placing it where it counts

Location matters as much as the post itself. Place at least one scratcher close to where your cat likes to snooze, and put posts right next to the furniture they're currently targeting — as "legal" alternatives within paw's reach.

Then make the post irresistible: scent posts with catnip, hang toys on them, or run a wand toy over the post during playtime so your cat discovers the texture mid-pounce.

Step 2: make the furniture boring

While the new post earns its reputation, make the old targets unappealing. You're not punishing — you're removing the reward:

  • Place double-sided sticky tape on furniture where they scratch. Cats hate the tacky feeling on their paws.
  • Place upside-down vinyl carpet runners (nubby side up) on furniture or on the floor where your cat stands to scratch.
  • Try vinyl scratch-guard protectors that fit over couch corners — discreet, and they protect while the retraining happens.
  • Temporarily cover or turn speakers toward the wall if those are targets.

Keep deterrents temporary. Once your cat has a solid post habit — usually a few weeks — you can remove the tape and guards. Most cats don't go back.

One more note: if increased scratching comes with other sudden changes — hiding, appetite loss, avoiding the litter box — ask your veterinarian to rule out medical causes before treating it as a behavior problem.

What about declawing?

Let's address the question directly, because it still comes up: the ASPCA is strongly opposed to declawing cats.

Declawing isn't a manicure — it's the amputation of the last digital bone, including the nail bed and claw, on each front toe. The surgery carries risks of anesthesia complications, excessive bleeding, infection, and chronic pain if bone fragments are left behind.

And it doesn't reliably solve the problem anyway. The humane alternatives work better: regular nail trims to blunt the tips, appealing scratching posts, double-sided tape deterrents, and soft temporary nail caps. Every one of them preserves the cat's health while protecting your furniture — which is the actual goal.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my cat scratch the couch and not the scratching post?

Because the couch is currently the better post — sturdier, taller, better texture, better location. Once you figure out your cat's preference for scratching, provide posts of that kind (watch what they target), place it right next to the couch, and make the couch temporarily unappealing with sticky tape. Most cats switch within a few weeks.

How do I get my cat to use a scratching post?

Scent the post with catnip, hang toys on it, and place it where your cat already likes to scratch. Run a wand toy over it during play so they discover the texture. Reward with praise or a treat when they use it. Never punish scratching elsewhere — just redirect.

Does declawing stop furniture scratching?

It removes the claws but not the behavior — and the ASPCA strongly opposes declawing because it's an amputation with real risks of chronic pain. Redirection with proper posts works better and keeps your cat healthy.

What can I put on furniture to stop scratching?

Double-sided sticky tape, upside-down vinyl carpet runners (nubby side up), and fitted vinyl scratch guards all work. Use them temporarily while your cat builds the new post habit, then remove them.

My cat scratches me when playing — is that the same thing?

Related but different — that's play aggression, not territorial scratching. Keep hands out of the "toy" category (use wand toys instead), and end play sessions before your cat gets overstimulated. The redirection principle is the same: give those claws an approved target.
