Leash Training: From First Walk to No Pulling

There's a special kind of arm ache that only puppy owners know. It's the one you get from being towed down the sidewalk by twelve pounds of pure enthusiasm, while your puppy acts like the leash is a personal insult and every squirrel is a long-lost friend.

Here's the reframe that changes everything: your puppy isn't being stubborn, dominant, or bad. Puppies aren't born knowing what a leash is — walking politely on one is a trained skill, and right now your puppy simply hasn't learned it yet. Pulling works for them: it gets them to the interesting smell faster. Every time it works, the habit gets a little stronger.

The good news is that the fix is well understood, and it doesn't involve yanking, jerking, or special collars. This guide walks you through the reward-based method that the AKC, the ASPCA, and veterinary behaviorists recommend — the gear, the 4-step method, the two moves that fix pulling mid-walk, and what to do when it all falls apart (because at some point, it will).

Why your puppy pulls (it's not defiance)

Dogs pull for one beautifully simple reason: it works. Your puppy wants to reach the bush, the person, the other dog — and leaning into the leash gets them there. From the puppy's point of view, pulling is a successful strategy, so they keep using it. Nothing about this is defiance. Leash walking is a trained skill that you will need to teach and continue to reinforce as your puppy grows.

It also helps to know what you're actually aiming for. Polite leash walking isn't a formal heel like you'd see in obedience competitions — that's a more advanced skill you can teach later. For now, the goal is simpler: your puppy walks near you on a loose leash without dragging you. That relaxed standard is the whole game. You're not training a show dog; you're training a pleasant walking companion.

The gear that makes training easier

You don't need much, but the right setup removes friction before you start.

Leash: use a lightweight 6-foot leash, and avoid retractable leashes — they teach the puppy that tension means more freedom, which is the exact opposite of what you want, and they give you less control.

Harness vs. collar: you can attach the leash to either, but a harness is generally a better option for puppies, so there's no pressure on their neck if they pull. Make sure it fits well enough that the puppy can't slip out but can move freely. For large puppies, a front-clip harness is recommended — the leash attaches at the chest, so pulling gently turns the puppy back toward you instead of giving them sled-dog leverage.

Treats and a marker: treats are what motivate your dog to work, so have lots of small treats your puppy is excited about. A treat pouch on your waist beats digging in pockets mid-walk. A clicker is optional — a cheerful marker word like "yes!" works just as well.

Start before the first real walk

Here's the part most owners skip: it's best to begin leash training as soon as you bring your puppy home — you can start even if your puppy isn't fully vaccinated and isn't going out on walks yet. The living room is the perfect classroom: zero distractions, and every lesson is short.

Charge the marker. Your puppy needs to learn that the click (or your marker word) means a treat is coming. In a quiet room, click or say "yes," then immediately give a treat. After a few repetitions, your puppy will start looking to you expectantly when they hear it. That glance — that is the foundation of everything that follows.

Reward attention. Clip the leash on and let it drag (supervised). Mark and reward any time your puppy offers you eye contact or attention while the leash is attached. You're building a simple association: noticing you pays well. By building value through checking in with us, dogs are likely to offer this behavior in the future when out on walks, and they'll naturally want to stay close by.

The 4-step loose-leash method

Once your puppy understands the marker and offers attention indoors, start walking.

Step 1 — Reward the position. Stand next to your puppy with the leash in a loose loop. Give treats at the level of your knee or hip as you walk forward — you're paying for the spot beside you, so make that spot the best place in the world to be. At first, reward every single step.

Step 2 — Add movement and the turn. Take one step forward and encourage your puppy to follow, rewarding as they catch up. When they run in front of you, simply turn in the opposite direction, call them to you, and reward them in place — then continue. The turn isn't a punishment; it's information. Forging ahead makes the walk change direction; staying close keeps it going.

Step 3 — Fade the treats. Gradually begin giving treats further apart: from every step to every other step, every third step, and so on. Don't rush this — dropping the reward rate too fast is the most common reason progress stalls. Mix in praise and the occasional jackpot treat so the puppy never quite knows when the big payoff comes.

Step 4 — Take it outside. When your puppy understands loose-leash walking inside your house or in the yard, you're ready to introduce the distractions of being outside. Bring high-value treats, keep the first outdoor sessions very short, and expect a temporary step backward — new environments are hard. That's normal, not failure.

The two moves that fix pulling mid-walk

No matter how good the training goes, your puppy will pull on real walks. When it happens, you have two moves. Both teach the same lesson: pulling stops forward motion, and a loose leash restores it.

1. Be a tree. The instant the leash goes tight, stop dead. Don't yank back, don't drag forward — just stop. Wait for any slack: the puppy may sit, look back at you, or wander sideways. The moment the leash loosens, say your marker and start moving again. Every single time the leash tightens and you keep walking anyway, you're teaching the puppy that pulling works. Consistency here is everything.

2. The penalty turn. When your puppy forges ahead, simply turn in the opposite direction, call them to you, and reward them in place. This is especially useful for puppies who blow through the "be a tree" stop because the distraction ahead is too exciting. Turning makes you the most interesting thing happening.

A note on the turn: teach a walk-with-me cue like "Let's go" or "With me" to tell your dog you want them to walk with you, and use it when you resume. Clear communication about where you're going keeps the puppy from feeling dragged along — when it's time to turn, get your dog's attention by saying their name, then introduce the cue as you turn and reward them for staying with you.

Let them sniff (seriously)

This one feels counterintuitive, but if you want your puppy to pull less, give them more opportunities to sniff and choose where they walk. Aim for a few sniffy walks a week where the puppy explores and picks the direction — a long line in a safe area is perfect for this.

Why does it help? Sniffing is mental work, and a puppy whose nose has had a workout is a calmer, more focused puppy on structured walks. It also removes the scarcity problem: a puppy who never gets to sniff will fight for every smell on every walk. Build sniff breaks into regular walks too — let them investigate, then give the cue "Let's go!" in a happy voice and reward them for coming back into position and walking with you.

What not to use

A quick word on equipment marketed as pulling "solutions": prong collars, choke chains, and shock collars. The professional consensus here is unusually clear. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior recommends that only reward-based training methods be used for all dog training, and that aversive methods — including prong collars, choke chains, electronic collars, and leash corrections — should not be used under any circumstances. Their position: there is no evidence that aversive methods are more effective than reward-based methods in any context, and they damage both animal welfare and the human-animal bond.

This isn't just ethics — it's practical. Pain-based tools suppress pulling in the moment without teaching the puppy what to do instead, and they can create fallout like fear or reactivity on walks. If your puppy is strong enough that pulling is a safety issue, the answer is a well-fitted front-clip harness plus help from a qualified reward-based trainer — not a harsher collar. Seeking professional support is never a sign of failure, and a basic puppy class like the AKC S.T.A.R. Puppy program is a great place to build these skills with guidance.

Your first two weeks: a simple plan

You don't need hour-long sessions. Short, frequent, and fun beats long and drilling — always end while your puppy still wants more.

Days 1–3: indoors, no walking required. Charge the marker word. Reward eye contact with the leash on. Let the puppy drag the leash (supervised) and discover it's no big deal.

Days 4–7: indoor walking. Reward position at your knee, one step at a time. Introduce the turn when the puppy forges ahead. Start fading treats from every step to every few steps.

Week 2: the quiet outdoors. Same game, new venue: a boring stretch of sidewalk or the yard. High-value treats. Very short sessions. Add one sniffy walk where the puppy chooses the route.

The ongoing rules: never let pulling work — not once, not "just to get home faster." Keep sessions short enough that you can end on a win. And if you're stuck or frustrated, there's no need to wait until you're struggling to work with a trainer — starting early can prevent bad habits from forming. If anything about your puppy's behavior worries you — pain, fear, or sudden changes — ask your veterinarian to rule out medical causes before assuming it's a training problem.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my puppy pull so hard on the leash?

Because it works — pulling gets them to interesting things faster, which reinforces the habit. Puppies aren't born knowing how to walk on a leash; it's a trained skill. The fix is teaching them that slack in the leash is what makes the walk continue, through consistent stops and turns paired with rewards for staying close.

How long does it take to leash train a puppy?

Expect weeks of short daily sessions, not days. Patience is key — teaching loose-leash walking, especially around distractions, takes time for puppies to fully understand. Most owners see real progress within 2–4 weeks of consistent practice, with reliability around distractions taking longer. Regression is normal; go back a step and keep going.

Should I use a collar or a harness for leash training?

A harness is generally the better option for puppies so there's no pressure on their neck if they pull — and for large or strong puppies, a front-clip harness is recommended. Either way, fit matters: snug enough to prevent escape, loose enough not to restrict movement. Avoid retractable leashes regardless of what the leash attaches to.

My puppy bites the leash on walks — how do I stop that?

Redirect, don't punish. Ask for an alternate behavior like sit, or lure with treats held near the nose so the puppy releases the leash, then praise and reward. Reward generously — every one to two seconds — for walking calmly without grabbing the leash, and keep the leash loose with less slack so it's less tempting as a toy.

Are prong collars or choke chains okay for a puppy that pulls?

No. Veterinary behaviorists recommend only reward-based training methods for all dog training, and advise that aversive tools including prong collars, choke chains, and leash corrections should not be used under any circumstances. If pulling is a safety concern, use a well-fitted front-clip harness and work with a qualified reward-based trainer instead.
