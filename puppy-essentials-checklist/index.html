What to Buy Before Bringing a Puppy Home: The Honest List

Pet stores want you to believe you need everything on the shelf. You don't. Here's the list we give friends: what your puppy genuinely needs before coming home, what to skip, and what can wait — from people who earn nothing from your choices.

The non-negotiables

Buy these before pickup day. No substitutes, no waiting.

  • Crate — wire crate with a divider panel, sized for their adult weight. See our crate training guide for setup.
  • Food — a bag of whatever the breeder or shelter has been feeding. Switch foods gradually later, not on day one.
  • Bowls — two, stainless steel or ceramic. Skip plastic (it harbors bacteria and some dogs react to it).
  • Collar + ID tag — lightweight, with your phone number. Fit: two fingers underneath.
  • Leash — a standard 6-foot leash. Not retractable — they teach pulling and are harder to control.
  • Enzyme cleaner — for accidents. Regular cleaners mask odor to you but not to the puppy, who will return to the same spot.
  • Treats — small, soft, pea-sized. Training runs on these.

Strongly recommended

  • Baby gates — to section off a puppy-safe area. Cheaper than replacing chewed furniture.
  • Chew toys — 3–4 different textures (rubber, rope, soft). Rotate them to keep interest up.
  • Grooming basics — brush suited to their coat type, nail clippers, puppy shampoo.
  • Poop bags — buy in bulk; you'll use hundreds.

What to skip (for now)

  • Expensive beds — puppies chew and have accidents. A washable towel in the crate is fine for months.
  • Wardrobe — coats and sweaters can wait until you know their adult size and whether they actually get cold.
  • Elaborate toy collections — start with a few; learn what your puppy likes before stocking up.
  • Premium food subscriptions — settle them in on familiar food first; optimize nutrition later with your vet's input.

The non-shopping list

Some of the most important preparation costs nothing:

  1. Book the first vet visit within 2–3 days of arrival.
  2. Puppy-proof one room — cables up, chemicals locked, small swallowable objects gone.
  3. Agree on house rules with everyone in the home: where the puppy sleeps, who's on potty duty, what words you'll use for commands. Consistency from day one saves months of retraining.
  4. Plan time off if possible — even a long weekend together makes the first week dramatically easier.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to prepare for a puppy?

For the genuine essentials — crate, food, bowls, leash, collar, ID tag, a few toys, enzyme cleaner, and treats — most owners spend roughly $200 to $400 before the puppy arrives, depending on size and where you shop. Our costs guides break this down further.

Should I buy premium everything upfront?

No. Puppies outgrow things fast and destroy plenty. Buy the safe essentials now; upgrade later once you know your dog's size, chewing style, and preferences.

Do I need a vet appointment before the puppy arrives?

Yes — a vet visit within the first few days is one of the most important items on any new-puppy list. Book it before pickup day if you can.
