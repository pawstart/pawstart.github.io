Best Crates for Puppies in Small Apartments (2026 Guide)

A puppy crate is non-negotiable — but in a small apartment, every square foot is contested territory. The wrong crate dominates your living room, clashes with everything, and gets replaced in three months when your puppy outgrows it. The right one folds flat, looks intentional, and lasts from 8 weeks to adulthood.

This guide won't give you a fake ranked list of products we never touched. Instead, here's what actually matters when you're choosing a crate for a puppy in a small space: the four crate types honestly compared, how to measure once and buy once, and which type fits your situation. For the training side — how to get your puppy to actually love the crate — see our crate training guide.

What actually matters in a small apartment

Most crate advice assumes you have a spare room. You don't. So judge every option against these five criteria:

  • Right size, not just small size. A crate that's too big slows potty training; too small is miserable. Size is about your puppy's body, not your floor plan.
  • It folds flat or earns its footprint. If it can't collapse, it should double as furniture — otherwise it's a permanent obstacle.
  • Right type for your puppy's temperament. A power chewer and a sleepy lapdog need different crates.
  • A divider panel. This is the single feature that lets you buy one crate instead of three as your puppy grows.
  • Looks and noise you can live with. As WebMD notes, if the crate sits in a common area where your dog stays near you, an attractive design genuinely matters — you'll see it every day.

No type is "best" in the abstract — the right one is the one that matches your puppy and your space. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

How to size it once (the divider trick)

Here's the method the AKC recommends: with your dog standing, measure from the top of the head to the bottom of the feet for height, then from the tip of the nose to the tip of the tail for length. Add 3 to 4 inches to each measurement — that's your crate's interior dimensions.

The rule underneath the math: your dog needs enough room to sit, stand, and lie down comfortably — but a crate that's too large can encourage your dog to eliminate inside, since they can sit away from the mess. Cornell's veterinary guidance puts it plainly: size for the adult dog, with enough room to stand without ducking the head, turn around, and lie down comfortably.

And here's the trick that saves you buying three crates: a divider panel lets a large crate fit your growing puppy — without it, a too-large kennel lets the puppy use one side as a bed and the other as a toilet, which works against potty training. Wire crates often include these divider panels: you buy the adult size once, then move the panel to expand the space as your puppy grows. For a quick sanity check on what "adult size" means for your breed, WebMD's weight-based starting points run from 18-inch crates for the smallest breeds (Chihuahuas, Pomeranians) up to 48–72 inches for the largest (Boxers, Huskies) — but always measure your individual dog, since body shape varies.

Which type for your situation

Match the crate to your life, not to a ranking:

  • The renter who needs it gone by day. A folding wire crate. It collapses flat in under a minute and slides behind a sofa or under a bed — the single biggest space win on this list.
  • The style-conscious. A furniture-style crate that doubles as an end table, or a wire crate with a fitted fabric cover. Both disappear into a living room; the cover has the added benefit of giving den-seeking dogs comfort — Cornell recommends covering a wire kennel while leaving one or two sides open.
  • The power chewer. Wire, full stop. Metal crates are the AKC's pick for dogs who chew through other materials, and soft-sided crates are off the table entirely.
  • The frequent traveler. A plastic airline-style crate — durable, easy to clean, and airline-approved if you fly. The ASPCA recommends keeping dogs crated in the car for everyone's safety, so a travel-ready crate pulls double duty.

Where to put it in a small apartment

Placement matters as much as the crate itself. The AKC advises placing the crate where your family hangs out, so your dog can be near you — in an apartment, that's usually a corner of the living room rather than a bedroom down the hall.

A few placement rules for tight spaces: keep it out of high-traffic lanes where it'll get kicked; keep it away from radiators and drafty windows, since wire crates offer the most air circulation while plastic offers the least, and temperature swings hit harder in a small room. If you're covering a wire crate, leave one or two sides uncovered so airflow and visibility remain. And if floor space is truly zero, remember the fold-flat trick: set the crate up for naps and nights, collapse it when you need the room.

Crate safety checklist

Before your puppy's first night in the new crate, run through these:

  • Collar off when unattended. Remove your puppy's collar before they're left alone in the crate — collars can catch on wire and become a strangulation hazard.
  • Divider secured. Make sure the divider panel locks firmly in place so your puppy can't squeeze behind it.
  • Time limits respected. A common rule of thumb is about one hour of crate time per month of age, and a crate should never be used as punishment. If you're gone a full workday, arrange a midday break or a dog walker — puppies can't hold it for 8 hours.
  • Watch for distress. Dogs that are excessively barking, pacing, panting, or drooling when confined should never be forced to remain crated — an exercise pen or a pet sitter may suit them better, and your veterinarian or a qualified trainer can help. If you're unsure, ask your veterinarian.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What size crate does my puppy need?

Measure your standing puppy from head-top to feet and nose-tip to tail-tip, then add 3 to 4 inches to each measurement. The crate should let them stand, turn around, and lie down — nothing more. Buying the adult size with a divider panel means you only buy once.

Are wire crates or plastic crates better for a small apartment?

Wire, for most apartment dwellers: they fold relatively easily for storage and usually include divider panels. Plastic wins if you fly with your dog, but it's more cumbersome to store — a real drawback when space is tight.

Do I need to buy multiple crates as my puppy grows?

No — not if you buy smart. Get a wire crate in your dog's expected adult size with an included divider panel, and expand the interior space as your puppy grows. One purchase covers puppyhood through adulthood.

Can I leave my puppy in the crate while I'm at work?

Not for a full workday without a break. The rule of thumb is about one hour per month of age, so an 8-hour absence needs a midday visit or dog walker. Always remove the collar when leaving your puppy unattended in the crate.

Are furniture-style crates safe for puppies?

They're safe as enclosures, but they're a compromised pick for the puppy months: wood crates are heavier, harder to clean, and usually lack divider panels — and potty-training accidents are guaranteed. Many owners start with a folding wire crate and switch to furniture-style once the puppy is grown and house-trained.
