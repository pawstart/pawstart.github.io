New Kitten Checklist: Your First 7 Days

Bringing home a kitten is one of life's great joys — and one of its great exercises in patience. That tiny creature stepping out of the carrier is experiencing everything for the first time: new smells, new sounds, new people, new rules. How you handle the first seven days sets the tone for your entire relationship.

The good news: kittens are remarkably adaptable when you give them the right start. This checklist walks you through it day by day — what to buy before pickup day, how to set up the safe room, and what to expect as your kitten settles in. One principle runs through all of it: your kitten sets the pace, not you.

Before pickup day: the supplies checklist

Have everything ready before the kitten arrives. Before bringing your new cat home, make sure you already have the basic provisions it will need, including a litter box, food, and ideally, a scratching post.

SupplyWhat to get
Litter boxSimple, low-sided box a kitten can climb into easily
LitterUnscented, fine-textured litter
FoodKitten-formulated food (check for the AAFCO label); find out what they've been eating
BowlsSeparate food and water dishes
Scratching postSturdy post for the safe room
CarrierFor the ride home and future vet trips
Bed & blanketCozy bed; bring a familiar-smelling blanket if the shelter offers one
ToysA few safe toys, including something to chew

A few details matter more than you'd think. Kittens may need a box with sides low enough for them to enter easily, and most cats prefer unscented, fine-textured litter. Provide as many boxes as you have cats, plus one — so for one kitten, that's two boxes once they're roaming the house. And keep food and water dishes far away from the litter box; cats are fastidious and may reject food placed too close to their toilet.

On food: feed a well-balanced, nutritionally complete diet formulated for the kitten's life stage, and check the packaging to ensure the product meets the standards established by the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO). First, find out what your new cat has been eating — even if you don't expect to stay with that diet, continue feeding some of the old food as you gradually switch to the new. If you're unsure which food to pick, ask your veterinarian for a recommendation.

Kitten-proof the safe room

Pick one quiet room to be your kitten's starter territory — a spare bedroom or bathroom works well. Before the kitten arrives, sweep it for hazards:

  • Plants: lilies are especially toxic to cats and can cause life-threatening kidney failure if ingested even in tiny amounts. Remove any lilies from the home entirely, including cut-flower arrangements.
  • Chemicals: keep household cleaners, detergents, and disinfectants in a place your cats can't reach.
  • Cords and small objects: tuck away electrical cords, hair ties, rubber bands, and anything dangling or swallowable.
  • Toilet lids: close them — small kittens can fall in.
  • Hiding spots: provide a cardboard box or covered bed. Your kitten will hide; give them good options.

Day 1: the safe-room arrival

Set up the room completely before you open the carrier — litter box in one corner, food and water on the opposite side, scratching post, bed, and toys in place. Then open the carrier door and step back. Don't pull the kitten out; let them emerge on their own timeline. Some kittens march out in minutes. Others stay put for hours. Both are normal.

For the first twenty-four hours, give your kitten time to settle into a calm, quiet environment — don't overwhelm them with having to meet lots of new people and children too soon. Skip the welcome party. If possible, bring your kitten home with some bedding — something familiar helps when everything else around them is new.

Don't worry if they barely touch their food on day one. New-home nerves suppress appetite, and most kittens eat lightly for the first day or two before their confidence (and hunger) kicks in.

Days 2–3: let the kitten set the pace

This is the hardest part for new owners: doing less. Your instincts say cuddle; the kitten's instincts say hide under the dresser until the giant creatures prove trustworthy.

If your kitten is hiding, as tempting as it may be, don't try to coax them out. Instead, spend time in the same room reading or watching TV, and allow your kitten to come to you when they feel ready. Spending time on the floor at their level can make you more appealing to them, too.

Always interact with your kitten when they initiate it — you can invite them by offering an outstretched hand to sniff and rub on. Short, gentle sessions beat long ones. And the use of synthetic feline pheromone diffusers may also help your cat or kitten feel safer and secure whilst they settle in — an optional aid, not a necessity.

Days 4–5: food, litter, and routine

By mid-week, most kittens are eating normally and starting to show their real personality. Now is the time to lock in the routines that will carry you for years.

Litter duty: scoop out the litter box at least once a day and clean the entire box once a week — kittens may not use a litter box if they deem it too messy. If your kitten needs a little help, try placing them gently in the litter box after meals or play sessions — never force them inside, and praise them when they get it right.

Feeding: keep serving the food they came with, mixed with gradually increasing amounts of the new food if you're switching. Keep mealtimes predictable — kittens thrive on routine the same way they thrive on patience.

Scratching: if your kitten eyes the furniture, redirect them to the post early and praise the right choice. Our guide to the redirect method: Why Cats Scratch Furniture (and How to Redirect It).

Days 6–7: the vet visit and opening up the house

Book a veterinary checkup within the first week. When you adopt your new cat or kitten, ask about prior vaccinations, nutrition, parasite control, and grooming that he or she has received — bring those records to the appointment so your vet knows exactly where your kitten stands.

Use the visit to ask your veterinarian about the vaccine schedule going forward and anything else on your mind. There's no silly question in week one.

Once your kitten is confident in the safe room — eating well, playing, seeking you out — start opening up the rest of the house gradually, one area at a time. Let them retreat to the safe room whenever they want; it stays their home base until the whole house feels like theirs.

A note: this guide covers general first-week care only. For any health concern — not eating, lethargy, vomiting, diarrhea, or anything that worries you — ask your veterinarian promptly rather than waiting it out.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How long should my kitten stay in one room?

Until they're confident there — eating well, playing, and seeking you out, which is usually a few days to a week. For the first twenty-four hours, give your kitten time to settle into a calm, quiet environment, and expand their territory gradually from there. There's no prize for rushing it.

What if my kitten won't eat on the first day?

That's normal. New-home stress suppresses appetite, and most kittens eat lightly for a day or two. Don't worry if they don't show much interest in food initially. If your kitten hasn't eaten anything at all for more than 48 hours, ask your veterinarian — that's the line between nerves and a problem.

When should a new kitten see the vet?

Within the first week. When you adopt, ask about prior vaccinations, nutrition, and parasite control, and bring those records so your vet can plan the vaccine schedule going forward. Wondering what those early visits look like? Our puppy vaccination schedule guide walks through a typical first-year vaccine timeline — the kitten specifics differ, but the questions to ask your vet are the same.

Can I let my kitten explore the whole house right away?

It's better not to. A whole house is overwhelming for a small animal in a new place — that's why the safe-room method works. Start with one room, then open up additional areas one at a time once your kitten is confident, always letting them retreat to their home base.

My kitten hides all the time — is something wrong?

Almost certainly not — hiding is how kittens cope with newness. Don't try to coax them out; spend time nearby and allow your kitten to come to you when they feel ready. If hiding comes with not eating, lethargy, or other worrying signs, ask your veterinarian.
