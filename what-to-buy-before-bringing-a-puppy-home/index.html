What to Buy Before Bringing a Puppy Home: The Honest Checklist

The short answer: you need about twelve categories of basics before day one — a crate, puppy food, two bowls, a collar with ID tag, a leash, a bed, chew toys, grooming basics, and cleanup supplies. Everything else can wait until you know your puppy. This checklist separates the must-haves from the wait-and-sees, based on what the AKC and government puppy guides actually list. One promise up front: no brand hype here, and no made-up prices. Prices change by region and season, so I'll tell you what to look for instead of pretending a number I write today stays true.

The must-haves: buy these before day one

Sleep and containment

  • Crate. A crate is one of the first housetraining tools the AKC lists — it helps reinforce a schedule from day one. Size it so your puppy can stand, turn, and lie down; if you buy big for growth, get one with a divider. (Our full guide: how to crate train a puppy at night.)
  • Bed or blankets. Your puppy needs their own bed — it becomes their special sleeping spot fast. Washable is the only feature that matters at this stage; skip the luxury mattress until the chewing phase passes.
  • Baby gate or playpen (optional but useful). The AKC suggests creating boundaries early — restricting access to certain rooms with puppy gates while your puppy learns the rules.

Food and water

  • Puppy food — the same one your breeder or rescue uses. A typical puppy should be fed two to three times a day, and you should consult your breeder or veterinarian about the diet. Buy a small bag of the current food first; you can transition to your preferred brand gradually over a week or two. Sudden food switches and new-home stress are a rough combination.
  • Two bowls. The government pre-puppy checklist keeps it simple: two bowls — one for water, one for food. Stainless steel or ceramic beats plastic (easier to sanitize, harder to chew).
  • Training treats. Small, soft, smelly. You'll use hundreds in the first month, so buy a decent-sized bag — they're your salary budget for good behavior.

Walking and ID

  • Collar and ID tag. A collar, lead, and ID tags are on every official checklist — put your phone number on the tag before the puppy ever steps outside.
  • Leash. A standard 4–6 foot leash. Skip retractable leashes for now — they teach pulling and give you less control around traffic.
  • Poop bags. The AKC lists poop bags among the housetraining basics. Buy the big box; you'll use them for years.

Teeth and play

  • Chew toys for teething. Hard rubber toys, rope toys, and flavored synthetic bones are particularly good for unsupervised play. Teething puppies chew constantly — legal outlets now prevent furniture casualties later. (Related: how to stop puppy biting.)
  • A few play toys. Soft stuffed toys and tennis balls are fine as long as you supervise — a determined puppy can tear into them and swallow pieces. Three or four toys is plenty to start; you'll learn what your puppy actually likes before buying more.

Cleanup and grooming: the unglamorous essentials

  • Enzymatic cleaner + paper towels. Stock up on paper towels, cleaning cloths, and commercial cleaners designed for dog messes. Regular household cleaners don't fully remove the scent markers that draw puppies back to the same spot.
  • Puppy pads (apartments especially). Puppy pads are listed as housetraining basics for getting started early. Even with a yard, a few pads for the first nights can save your floors.
  • Grooming basics. Your coat type dictates the brush, but the starter kit is the same: brush/comb, dog shampoo, nail clippers, and dog toothpaste with a toothbrush. Dog dental wipes are a good low-effort way to start dental care. Never use human toothpaste or shampoo on a dog — they're formulated differently.

The 48-hour setup workflow

  1. Set the stations. Crate in your bedroom (see our nighttime crate guide), food and water in a quiet corner away from foot traffic, a small gated safe zone for unsupervised moments.
  2. Puppy-proof sweep. The government checklist stresses a safe, secure environment: get down at ground level and check for drain holes, dangerous items, and anything chewable at puppy height. Cords up, trash secured, toilet lids down, houseplants out of reach.
  3. Book the vet. Arrange microchipping, vaccinations, and worming — ask your vet for advice rather than guessing. Book the first visit within the first week, before you need one urgently.
  4. Stash the treats everywhere. Treat pouch by the door, jar in the kitchen, pocket supply for walks. Training opportunities appear without warning in week one.

Money talk: what this honestly costs

You'll notice there's no price list in this article. That's deliberate: prices vary widely by region, retailer, and season, and any number I print today would be wrong somewhere by next month. What I can tell you honestly:

  • The crate and food are your two biggest supply costs. Bigger dog, bigger crate, bigger food bill — that's the main variable.
  • The hidden costs dwarf the shopping list: first vet visit, vaccination series, and a puppy training class will likely cost more than all the gear combined. Budget for those first.
  • Compare before you buy. Check current prices at two or three retailers for the big items (crate, food). For small stuff, don't over-optimize — your time is worth more than saving a dollar on poop bags.

What NOT to buy (yet)

  • Retractable leashes, choke or prong collars. They work against the training you're about to do.
  • Adult dog food. Puppy food is formulated for growth — consult your breeder or vet about diet rather than grabbing whatever's on sale.
  • A mountain of toys. Start with a few; observe; then buy what your individual puppy actually enjoys.
  • Human shampoo or toothpaste for the dog. Wrong pH, wrong ingredients. Dog-formulated only.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

How much should I budget for puppy supplies?

We won't give you a fake number. The honest answer: the initial gear (crate, bed, bowls, collar, leash, toys, grooming, cleaners) is the smaller part — the first vet visits, vaccines, and a training class usually cost more. Price the big items at two or three current retailers and add a buffer for the vet.

What can I wait to buy until later?

Almost everything comfort-related: the fancy bed, the toy collection, extra leashes, grooming gadgets. What can't wait: crate, food, collar/leash/ID, chew toys, enzymatic cleaner, and a vet appointment.

Should I buy the same food the breeder was using?

Yes — at least for the first bag. The AKC advises consulting your breeder or veterinarian about your puppy's diet, since puppies can need special formulations. Transition gradually over 7–10 days once your puppy is settled.

Do I need pet insurance before the puppy arrives?

You don't need it on day one, but research it in week one — most policies have waiting periods, so the coverage you buy today doesn't start tomorrow. We couldn't verify which provider fits every situation, so compare waiting periods, exclusions, and deductibles yourself rather than trusting a ranked list.

What's the single most useful thing to buy first?

The crate. It's the only item that simultaneously handles safe sleep, housetraining structure, and a calm retreat — and you'll use it every single day for months. Size it right and introduce it positively from day one.
