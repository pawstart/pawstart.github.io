How to Puppy-Proof Your Apartment: Room by Room

A puppy does not walk into your apartment and admire the decor. A puppy walks in and starts tasting it. Cords, shoes, plants, trash, the pill you dropped under the nightstand — to a puppy, everything at nose height is worth investigating with its mouth. That is normal puppy behavior, and it is exactly why proofing matters before the puppy arrives, not after the first emergency.

Puppies are endlessly curious and energetic, so proofing has to be thorough — a single puppy can find trouble in places you would never expect. In an apartment the stakes are a little higher than in a big house: the kitchen, the plants, the medications, and the balcony are all within a few steps of each other, and there is nowhere for a hazard to hide. The good news is that a small space is also faster to proof. One focused afternoon, room by room, covers nearly all of it.

This guide walks through each room in order, flags the hazards that actually send puppies to the vet, and ends with a toxic-plant check and a plan for the moment something gets eaten anyway. It is general information for new owners, not veterinary advice — and where it matters, it will tell you to call your vet rather than guess.

Start at puppy eye level

Before you move a single thing, get down on your hands and knees and crawl through each room slowly. You are looking for what a curious puppy will find: dangling cords, gaps behind furniture, small objects on the floor, low shelves, open bags. Ask one question in every room: what could a puppy chew, swallow, pull down, climb into, or knock over?

Do the walkthrough twice. Once at the height of a small puppy, and once imagining a puppy three months older, taller, and able to jump onto a low chair and from there onto a table. Puppies grow fast, and a shelf that is safe this week may not be safe next month. That is why proofing is not a one-time job — a quick sweep once a month, and another sweep every time your puppy learns a new skill like jumping or opening a low cabinet, keeps the apartment safe as they grow.

One more ground rule: do not give a new puppy the run of the whole apartment on day one. Open up rooms gradually with baby gates, starting with the puppy-safe zone described below. Fewer rooms means fewer hazards to watch, and it makes house training easier too.

The kitchen

If one room deserves the most attention, it is this one. The kitchen concentrates food, trash, chemicals, and cords in a small space, and it is where puppies do their most determined counter-surfing.

Food first. Some foods can seriously sicken pets even in small amounts — chocolate, grapes, raisins, onions, garlic, macadamia nuts, and xylitol, the sweetener found in sugar-free gum, candy, baked goods, and some peanut butters. Keep all of it off counters and out of low cabinets, and remember that a puppy can learn to surf a counter sooner than you expect. Table scraps are off the menu, with sugar-free gum, chocolate, and raisins among the foods that are particularly dangerous for dogs.

Trash. Garbage smells draw puppies in, and bin contents can upset their stomachs or even poison them, so bins need securing. In an apartment kitchen, that means a bin with a locking lid or a bin inside a latched cabinet. An open pedal bin is a puzzle your puppy will solve.

Under the sink. Store cleaning products up high or behind childproof latches, and keep your puppy out of the room while you use them, since chemical fumes can affect them. Dishwasher pods, oven cleaner, and drain cleaner all live here — treat this cabinet like a locked medicine cabinet.

Cords and small items. Cords should be unplugged, moved out of reach, or run through concealers, because a chewing puppy risks mouth burns or electric shock. That covers toaster and kettle cords, phone chargers on the counter, and the blender cord dangling over the edge. Sweep the floor for the small stuff too: small objects like coins, paper clips, rubber bands, and jewelry should be put away, since they can choke a puppy. Twist ties, bottle caps, and magnets belong in the same category.

The living room

The living room is where your puppy will spend most of its time, so it is where the most everyday objects become hazards.

Cords and cables. TV cables, lamp cords, game controllers, and chargers all dangle at puppy height. Run them through cord concealers, behind furniture, or up high — the same rule as the kitchen, applied everywhere a cord hangs. Doors and windows should stay closed so a puppy can't slip out or fall, and blind cords need securing so they can't wrap around a puppy's neck. Blind cords are easy to forget and genuinely dangerous; wrap them up high with a cleat or tie them out of reach.

Plants. Poisonous houseplants should be moved where a puppy can't nibble them. The living room is where most houseplants live, so this is where the toxic-plant check in the next section pays off. If you are not sure about a plant, move it out of the room until you have checked it — a hanging planter or a high shelf buys you time.

Everything on the floor. Shoes, socks, bags, remote controls, kids' small toys, and anything in a coat pocket that lands on a chair. Puppies do not distinguish between their toys and your things, and soft items like socks are the classic swallow hazard. Give your puppy its own toys that are too big to swallow, and give everything else a home above puppy height. Sharp objects — knives, scissors, razors, tools — need to stay out of a puppy's reach. That includes the craft scissors on the coffee table and the letter opener by the mail pile.

Unstable furniture. A wobbly bookshelf, a plant stand, or a leaning floor lamp can all come down on a puppy that jumps up or pulls at them. Anchor tall furniture to the wall if you can, and move anything top-heavy away from the puppy's main play area.

The bathroom

Small room, concentrated hazards. Keep the bathroom door closed when you are not in it — that single habit solves half of this section.

Medications. Human medicines and human food are the two leading causes of pet poisoning, so prescription and over-the-counter medicines need secure storage — including ones tucked into backpacks, purses, or nightstands. Medications don't belong on low tables, bathroom counters, or nightstands — even in pill bottles — because a puppy can easily reach them there. A pill bottle is not childproof against a determined puppy's teeth. Vitamins, painkillers, and antidepressants all belong in a high, closed cabinet.

Toilet and water. Toilet lids should stay closed to stop a puppy drinking from the bowl or tumbling in. Toilet-bowl cleaner residue is the reason this matters beyond the mess — a closed lid is a zero-effort fix.

Sharp and chemical. Razors, tweezers, and nail scissors go in a closed drawer, not on the counter edge. Bathroom cleaners, bleach, and air fresheners go behind a latched cabinet door, just like the kitchen cleaners.

The bedroom

The bedroom feels safe because it is quiet. It is not — it is where laundry, chargers, and nightstand clutter collect at exactly puppy height.

Laundry. Treat the laundry pile like a hazard zone. Socks and underwear are the items puppies swallow most often in bedrooms, and a closed hamper with a lid costs almost nothing. Do a floor check every night: if it is small enough to fit in a puppy's mouth, it goes in the hamper or the closet.

The nightstand. This is the bedroom version of the bathroom counter: medications, a pack of gum, a phone charger, a glass of water, loose change. Move medications to a high cabinet, keep gum (xylitol risk) in a drawer, and route charger cables behind the nightstand rather than across the floor.

Bags and backpacks. Medicines stashed in backpacks, purses, or nightstands still pose a poisoning risk and need proper storage. Hang bags on a hook by the door instead of dropping them on the bedroom floor, and check the side pockets — that is where loose pills and gum usually hide.

The balcony and shared spaces

Apartment living adds two spaces a house guide never covers: the balcony and the shared areas of the building.

Balcony railings. Check the gaps between balusters. A small puppy can squeeze through a gap that looks far too narrow, and a puppy that can fit its head through can usually get the rest of the way. Block gaps with balcony mesh or plexiglass panels, and never leave a young puppy unsupervised on a balcony — wind, a sudden noise, or a bird can send a puppy scrambling in a direction you did not expect. Very young puppies shouldn't be left outdoors unsupervised; outdoor time is for play and training under your watch, away from heat, predators, and other dangers. The same logic applies to a balcony: outside means supervised, always.

Shared hallways and elevators. Keep your puppy on a leash from the moment the front door opens. Elevator doors, stairwell doors, and neighbors' doors opening unexpectedly are all escape routes. A leash and a closed front door are the whole system here.

If you have a yard or garden. A yard fence should be tall enough to stop jumping and gap-free to stop crawling under, and toxic yard plants should be removed so a puppy doesn't nibble them. After a yard is treated with fertilizer, pesticides, or insecticides, keep your dog off it — those chemicals can seriously harm a puppy. If your building has a shared garden, ask when it was last treated before letting your puppy sniff around in it. And in-ground pools need their own fence: they are a major hazard for puppies, and a barrier stops accidental falls into the water.

Set up a puppy-safe zone

Proofing every room does not mean your puppy gets all of them at once. Pick one room — usually the living room or a kitchen corner — and make it the puppy-safe zone: a baby gate across the doorway, a bed, a water bowl, and chew toys that are too big to swallow. This is where your puppy stays whenever you cannot actively supervise, and it is where the crate goes if you are crate training.

The zone serves two purposes. First, it caps the number of hazards you have to watch at any moment. Second, it gives your puppy a predictable home base, which helps with settling in during the first weeks. As your puppy earns trust — reliable toilet habits, less mouthing of furniture — open up one new room at a time, proofing each one before it opens.

The toxic plant check

Plants deserve their own section because they are the hazard most new owners get wrong: the plant looks harmless, it came from a regular store, and nobody warned you. Check every plant you own against the ASPCA's list before your puppy arrives, and check every new plant before it comes through the door.

One rule covers everything not on this table: Unsure about a plant? Err on the side of caution and keep it away from your puppy. And remember the ASPCA's own caveat: the ASPCA notes its list covers the most commonly encountered plants and isn't exhaustive — absence from it doesn't prove a plant is safe. When in doubt, the plant stays out of the puppy's rooms.

If your puppy eats something they shouldn't

Proofing reduces the risk; it does not eliminate it. Puppies are fast, and something will get eaten eventually. When it happens, skip the guesswork and the internet remedies and go straight to a professional:

A suspected toxin exposure means getting help at once — call your vet or a poison hotline immediately, even if you're not sure what was eaten. Save these numbers in your phone now, before you need them: your regular vet, the nearest 24-hour emergency clinic, and the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435. Note what was eaten, how much, and when — that is the information the vet or hotline will ask for first.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

Should I puppy-proof before my puppy comes home, or after?

Before — ideally the weekend before arrival. Proofing takes an afternoon, and a new puppy starts exploring within minutes of walking in. Doing the puppy-eye-level walkthrough first means day one is spent settling in, not chasing a puppy away from a dangling cord.

Are baby gates enough to keep a puppy safe?

Gates are the tool, not the whole system. A gate keeps your puppy out of unproofed rooms, but the rooms they can access still need proofing, and a puppy still needs supervision — especially a young one. Think of it as gates plus proofing plus supervision, not gates instead of them.

Which houseplants are most dangerous for puppies?

Start with sago palm, lilies, azaleas, and oleander — all listed as toxic by the ASPCA or Cornell, with azaleas and oleander carrying fatal-risk warnings. But the honest answer is longer than any shortlist: the ASPCA's own list is not all-inclusive, so check every plant you own against it rather than trusting a top-ten list.

What should I do if my puppy eats something they shouldn't?

Call your vet or a poison hotline immediately, even when you're unsure what was eaten — acting fast can save a life. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is at 888-426-4435. Note what was eaten, how much, and when, and do not try home remedies while you wait — the professional on the phone will tell you what to do next.

Do I need to re-proof my apartment as my puppy grows?

Yes. A puppy that could not reach the kitchen counter at ten weeks may surf it at five months, and a puppy that ignored the bookshelf may discover it can pull a book down. Do a quick sweep once a month and another after every new skill — jumping, climbing, opening low cabinets — and move the goalposts before your puppy does.
